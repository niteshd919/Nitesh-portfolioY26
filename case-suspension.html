Suspension Diagnostics

Sensor based suspension diagnostic system on IVI

One clear decision, at the moment the driver can make it.

Status
Concept · not yet tested with drivers
Role
Product designer · self-initiated
Timeline
45-minute whiteboard, then two prototype iterations
Team
Solo
Platform

Tools

01 · The short version

Drivers don’t want data. They want a decision.

The problem statement asked for a predictive suspension health experience on a car’s center display. After 45 minutes at the whiteboard I had a different answer. A moving driver can’t read a screen of suspension data. They need to know if it’s serious and what to do, at a moment when they can act.

So the center display stays quiet while the car moves. Most findings wait until the car is parked and arrive as a single card: what’s happening, what it means, what to do, and why the car thinks so. Only a critical fault interrupts a drive, through the instrument cluster, a chime and a voice.

I then built it as a working IVI prototype and reworked it twice. The first iteration turned the whiteboard into code, and seeing it run overturned one of my own rules. The second added line guidance on rough ground: the car shows which way to steer, how far and how soon, so the suspension takes fewer hits.

This is a concept. Nothing has shipped and no driver has used it yet, so the page ends with the plan to test it, starting with usability testing.

02 · What’s broken today

Wear is gradual. The warning is all or nothing.

Today the car has one amber warning light and a fixed service schedule. Suspension wears out gradually, and faster on India’s speed breakers, potholes and monsoon roads, so drivers get used to it and stop noticing. When the light does come on, it says nothing about how serious things are. Some drivers panic. Others ignore it. For the carmaker, late catches mean more breakdowns and warranty claims.

The severity model, the three checks and the parked card, built into the IVI prototype. 47 automated checks cover every severity, snoozing, escalation, the monthly cap, offline booking and both service outcomes. The panel on the right is the prototype’s dev control, used to switch scenarios.

Iteration 2

Line guidance on rough ground

Five times a second, the car scores the scanned ground 22 m ahead for every line it could take, counting where all four wheels will roll. It asks for a move only when shifting at least 0.6 m cuts the suspension load by 55% or more. The countdown ends 0.8 s before the obstacle. If it’s too close to steer round, there’s no cue and the predictive pre-set takes the hit.

05 · What building it taught me

Seeing it run overturned one of my own rules.

No drivers yet. These came from reviewing the running prototype myself.

  1. The data earned its place back

    The board said evidence is never the default. Running it, every wheel’s state was worth a glance, so per-wheel readings now show by default when parked, beside the decision card. The data came back, but only for the moment a driver can read it.

  2. Point with the wheel, not the camera

    The camera used to turn the car toward an affected corner. Now each parked state has one fixed shot, framed so all four wheel cards fit, and a corner is pointed out by its own card and ring.

  3. Removing the cluster preview left a gap

    I took the cluster mock-up off the center display; the car’s real cluster is still the channel while driving. The cost: a critical alert has no visual on this screen. The chime, voice, screen-reader alert and report log carry it.

  4. Small screens need a priority

    At 1366 × 768 the four wheel cards don’t fit beside a decision card. The decision card wins, and the numbers move to All readings, one tap away.

  5. Fewer, bigger moves

    Looser thresholds for line guidance gave the same benefit with 70% more cues. I chose fewer, bigger moves: at least 0.6 m and 55% less load. That still means about 17 cues a minute on very rough ground.

  6. One exception to my own rule

    Line guidance shows on the center display while driving. I treat it as driving assistance, like a hazard callout, so the quiet-while-moving rule for health alerts doesn’t apply. It’s the riskiest call here, and the first thing to test.

06 · What the simulation shows

Fewer hits on a simulated trail. Not yet tested on a real driver.

  • 68% fewer rock strikes when following the cue than holding the line: 86 against 271 across three stretches
  • 66% less suspension load past the comfortable height, between 53% and 73% per stretch
  • 17 cues a minute on very rough ground. That’s the cost, and the number usability testing has to check

Measured in the prototype’s own simulation: its terrain, speed and steering, run headless over three 600 m stretches at 80% roughness, following the cue against holding the line. It shows the guidance logic works. Whether a driver can follow it safely is still untested.

07 · What I’d do next

Put it in front of drivers before building anything else.

Usability testing first

  • Parked card, with 10 owners: show it for five seconds, then ask what’s wrong, how serious it is and what they’d do. It passes if most can answer all three without help.
  • Driving simulator, for the critical alert and the steering cue: eye-tracked glances under 2 seconds and tasks under 12. Run the same trail with and without cues.
  • Line guidance at 17 cues a minute: do drivers follow, hesitate or tune it out? This decides whether the feature stays.
  • Severity labels: can owners tell Plan from Caution unaided, and does “Plan · not urgent” calm them or get ignored?
  • Service advisors: sit with them as they explain a report to a driver, including the false alarm where the dealer finds no fault.
  • Then a small pilot on test cars, tracking issues fixed before they fail, false alarms, alerts per car and glance time, before anything scales.

Who I’d bring in

  • Vehicle dynamics engineers.
  • Data science, to set the confidence bar that triggers an alert.
  • Functional safety and legal, for the liability wording.
  • The instrument-cluster HMI team.
  • Dealer operations.

Still open

  • What confidence should trigger an alert? 70% is a placeholder.
  • How do we say “Safe to drive” without liability risk? It lives in one constant, so legal can change it in one place.
  • What should a booking really cost? The ₹ figures are placeholders.
  • Should a critical alert show anything on the center display when audio is off?
  • Should a new steering cue also play a sound?
