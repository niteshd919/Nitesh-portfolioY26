      02The briefWhy

      14%

      Step 2 of 7, The brief: Step 02 / 07The brief A stepper turns one big task into small, finishable ones.

      Question Should we build one at all, and what decides whether people finish?

      Splitting a task is what helps people. Showing progress across the split helps only when that progress looks fast and honest.

      So I treated the stepper as two decisions: first whether to split a task at all, then how to show progress across it. This is phase one, desk research.

      What the split does

      1. Benefit 01

        Looks finishable

        A short screen looks small enough to finish.

      2. Benefit 02

        Fewer errors

        Each step is checked as people go.

      3. Benefit 03

        Shortest path

        Branching skips what doesn’t apply.

      Only when the split is real: three or more stages, a fixed order, one sitting.

      Sources: NN/g, Wizards; USWDS, Step indicator · Illustration is schematic

      03PerceptionPsychology

      29%

      Step 3 of 7, Perception: Step 03 / 07Perception People read a stepper as an estimate of effort.

      Question What does someone see when they glance at a progress indicator?

      “That took a minute, so this is about five.”

      The maths people do at a glance

      People see “Step 2 of 5”, remember how long the first step took and project the total. Slow early progress advertises a long task, and they leave before they reach the easy part.

      Motivation isn’t flat, so the steps can’t be either.

      Effort rises as a goal gets closer: the goal-gradient effect. Between a confident start and a quick finish, motivation sags, which researchers call being stuck in the middle. So the middle steps carry the least, and the finish line stays in view.

      Sources: Hull 1932; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006; Bonezzi et al. 2011

      A head start, at a car wash

      Same eight washes to go, and far more people finished.

      In a field study at a car wash, loyalty cards that started with two of ten stamps already filled were completed by 34% of customers. Blank eight-stamp cards: 19%. The remaining effort was identical.

      For a stepper, that means counting work people have already done, such as an account created or details pre-filled, as completed steps.

      On this pageArriving counted as step one. You started at 14%.

      Source: Nunes & Drèze 2006, the endowed progress effect

      04EvidenceExperiments

      43%

      Step 4 of 7, Evidence: Step 04 / 07Evidence A progress bar alone doesn’t cut drop-off. A slow start raises it.

      Question Does showing progress actually help people finish?

      In one survey experiment, the same questionnaire ran with four kinds of bar: one that raced ahead early and slowed later, one that moved at the true pace, one that crawled early and caught up at the end, and none at all. People saw the same questions. Only the bar changed, and so did how many of them quit.

      32 experiments agree

      Steady bars made no significant difference. Fast-start bars cut drop-off; slow-start bars raised it.

      A meta-analysis of 32 randomised experiments. People estimate the remaining effort from the bar, so heavy early steps advertise a long journey.

      And in the field

      GOV.UK’s Carer’s Allowance service removed a 12-step indicator, with no effect on completion rates or times. Its design system now advises testing a form without one first.

      RuleDon’t fake progress. Put the easy steps first, so honest progress moves fast early.

      Sources: Conrad, Couper, Tourangeau & Peytchev 2010, as reported by SoundRocket; Villar, Callegaro & Yang 2013; GOV.UK Design System, Question pages

      On this page: its own curve, at the top, is fast then slow, because its shortest steps come first. The dashed line is the same seven steps, longest first.

      The insight

      People don’t finish the indicator. They finish the steps.

      So design the steps first, then an indicator that describes them honestly. A fast start comes from easy steps placed first, never from a bar that runs ahead of the work.

      05PromisesTrust

      57%

      Step 5 of 7, Promises: Step 05 / 07Promises A stepper makes four promises. Breaking one costs trust.

      Question What do people expect it to tell them, and what breaks their trust?

      1. Promise 01

        The task has edges

        It’s finite, and this is how big it is.

      2. Promise 02

        The order is decided

        Just do the next thing: a relief for novices, a constraint for experts.

      3. Promise 03

        Your progress counts

        What you’ve done is kept, and going back costs nothing.

      4. Promise 04

        No surprises ahead

        The steps shown are the steps that exist.

      Three ways to break one

      Most stepper failures start with a broken promise.

      1. Failure 01

        A total that jumps

        It breaks the effort estimate people have just made.

      2. Failure 02

        A Back that loses data

        Re-entry fatigue, then abandonment.

      3. Failure 03

        A fee at review

        It destroys trust at the moment of commitment.

      EV drivers have a word for this: a range estimate that keeps jumping is a guess-o-meter, and nobody plans a trip on one. A step total that jumps is the same failure.

      Sources: Baymard Institute, Checkout UX; GOV.UK Design System; U.S. Web Design System · Mock-ups are illustrative

      What people expect

      Eight questions it answers without being asked

      1. Expectation 01

        How long will this take?

        State the number of steps, and the time if known, on the first screen.

        W3C WAI
      2. Expectation 02

        Where am I?

        The current step is the most prominent, with “Step 3 of 5” beside a real heading.

        USWDS
      3. Expectation 03

        What have I done?

        Completed steps show a check; finished sections collapse into readable summaries.

        Carbon · Baymard
      4. Expectation 04

        What happens next?

        The button names where it goes: “Continue to payment”, not a bare “Next”.

        Baymard · NN/g
      5. Expectation 05

        Can I go back safely?

        A visible Back, a working browser Back, every input kept, completed steps clickable.

        GOV.UK · Baymard
      6. Expectation 06

        Is something wrong?

        An error state on the step, plus inline field errors that say how to fix them.

        Carbon
      7. Expectation 07

        Can I stop and come back?

        Save each step, and resume at the same one.

        NN/g
      8. Expectation 08

        Do I have to do this?

        Label optional steps, and offer a skip.

        Material · W3C WAI
      How people use it

      Four ways people use a stepper

      Use 01

      As navigation

      They click completed steps to go back, even where that isn’t built. If it looks clickable, it must be.

      Use 02

      As a progress check

      They glance at it after every Continue to confirm they moved. A total that changes breaks trust.

      Use 03

      Barely, on phones

      Full horizontal bars often go unnoticed on small screens. A one-line “Step 3 of 5 · Payment” header works better.

      Use 04

      By ear

      Screen reader users never see the graphic. Progress belongs in the page title and the heading.

      Sources: Baymard Institute; U.S. Web Design System; GOV.UK; Red Hat; W3C WAI

      One component

      Four display modes, one state model

      1. Mode 01

        Horizontal bar

        Desktop and tablet, up to five short steps

      2. Mode 02

        Vertical side rail

        Desktop with six or more steps, long labels or sub-steps

      3. Step 3 of 5 Mode 03

        Compact header

        Every phone layout, any number of steps

      4. Mode 04

        Progress bar only

        Flows whose number of steps can change

      Seven step states, each in words and an icon, never colour alone
      • Not started
      • Current
      • Completed
      • Error
      • Optional
      • Skipped
      • Locked

      Built in, not added per flow: autosave on every step, clickable completed steps, accessible markup and analytics events.

      The bar for every design review

      Five rules the design is held to

      1. 1

        Earn it

        Steps only for three or more meaningful stages; otherwise a single page.

      2. 2

        Keep promises

        The real steps, a total that never jumps, and no surprises at review.

      3. 3

        Start fast

        Easy steps first, and count work the person has already done.

      4. 4

        Back is always safe

        Autosave every step; completed steps are clickable and lose nothing.

      5. 5

        Accessible by construction

        Progress in the page title and heading, states in words, focus on each new step.

      If a design breaks one of these, it gets challenged. Each rule traces back to the evidence in steps three to five.

      Task success and happiness

      Every metric names the levers that move it

      MetricWhat it tells usDesign levers
      Flow completionCan people finish the whole task?Fewer fields per step; easy steps first; save and resume
      Drop-off by stepWhich step to fix firstSplit or lighten the worst step; move heavy steps later
      Time to completeMedian time per step, and in totalAutofill, smart defaults, fewer screens
      Errors per stepWhere fields confuse peopleInline validation; specific messages; never clear input
      Back-navigationWhere labels or surprises misleadClearer labels; summaries; Edit links on review
      Taps on the indicatorWhether it looks clickable where it isn’tMake completed steps clickable; remove the look elsewhere
      Resume rateWhether saved flows come back within 7 daysSave state; a task-list hub on return; a reminder
      Perceived ease (SEQ)How hard it felt, against an average of about 5.5 out of 7Everything above; ask why for any rating under 5

      Targets come from a two-week baseline. The metrics cover two categories of Google’s HEART framework, task success and happiness, plus retention for flows that take more than one session.

      Sources: MeasuringU, Single Ease Question; Lyssna, Google HEART framework

      Experiment backlog

      First experiments, highest expected impact first

      1. Experiment 01

        Easy steps first against the current order. Tests the early-progress effect.

      2. Experiment 02

        Indicator or none on short flows, as GOV.UK advises.

      3. Experiment 03

        Compact header against a full horizontal bar, on phones.

      4. Experiment 04

        “Continue to payment” against a bare “Next”.

      5. Experiment 05

        Clickable completed steps against static ones.

      6. Experiment 06

        An endowed start, sign-up shown as step one done, against a blank start.

      Guardrails for every test: downstream errors or rejections, such as failed KYC checks, support contacts and the accessibility pass rate must not get worse. For low-traffic flows, run 5–8 moderated sessions per round with SEQ instead of an A/B test.

      Accessible by construction

      It works without sight, colour or a pointer

      1. Progress in the page titleStep 2 of 4: Shipping address – CheckoutW3C WAI
      2. Progress in the headingA real heading under the indicator, with the step in itW3C WAI · USWDS
      3. An ordered list of stepsCompleted steps are links; the current one carries aria-currentW3C WAI · USWDS
      4. State in wordsHidden “Completed”, “Current”, “Not completed”, never colour or an icon aloneWCAG 1.4.1
      5. Focus follows the stepOn a step change, focus moves to the new heading; on an error, to the summaryMy recommendation
      6. Targets of 24 px or moreEvery tappable step, at least 24 × 24 CSS pixelsWCAG 2.5.8
      7. No progress that moves by itselfStyle a native progress bar staticWCAG 2.2.2
      8. Readable pending stepsSteps to come keep their contrast and never look disabledWCAG 1.4.3 · 1.4.11

      Test plan: a keyboard-only pass, VoiceOver and TalkBack, 200% zoom and 320 px reflow, and at least two people who use assistive technology in every usability round.

      The ask

      Three decisions, then the road

      1. 1

        Approve the direction

        One adaptive stepper component, plus a task-list hub for long flows that take more than one session.

      2. 2

        Pick the first two flows

        And confirm each one’s number of steps, and whether answers can change it.

      3. 3

        Fund user validation

        Two rounds of 5–8 moderated sessions, plus baseline funnel data for the current flows.

      Open questions for the design phase
      • Which flows get the stepper first, and how many stages does each have?
      • Can any flow branch so that its total changes? That decides a fixed count or a progress bar.
      • Web, native apps or both? That decides the system Back gesture and the bottom sheet.
      • Is there a design system and a token set to extend?
      • Is baseline funnel data available for the current flows?
      • Do security or session time-out rules limit save and resume?
